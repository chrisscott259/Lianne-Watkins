Trauma doesn’t always look the way people expect.

It might look like a memory that comes back without permission. A smell, a sound, a moment in an otherwise ordinary day that pulls you somewhere you don’t want to go.

It might look like staying very busy so you never have to sit still with yourself. Or shutting down completely and feeling nothing at all.

It might look like a body that won’t relax. Muscles always braced. Sleep that doesn’t come, or comes and brings nightmares.

It might look like relationships that keep breaking in the same place, no matter how different the person is this time.

Trauma takes many forms. It doesn’t require a capital-T traumatic event. Prolonged stress, chronic emotional neglect, a relationship that left you questioning your own reality — these leave the same kind of marks. The nervous system doesn’t distinguish.

What makes EMDR different for trauma.

Most approaches to trauma ask you to talk about what happened. To narrate it, to process it verbally, to integrate it through understanding and language. This can help. But for many people, it only goes so far.

Trauma isn’t primarily stored in the part of the brain that processes words. It’s stored lower — in the body, in the nervous system, in the part of the brain that fires before thought.

EMDR reaches that level directly. Using bilateral stimulation while you hold a memory in mind, it helps the brain reprocess what got stuck — so it can finally be filed away as something that happened, rather than something that is happening.

You don’t have to describe everything. You don’t have to go through every detail. Many clients are relieved to find the process less verbally intensive than they feared — and more effective than they expected.

What Lianne brings to trauma work.

Lianne is trauma-informed at the level of how she sits with you, not just at the level of technique. She understands how trauma affects the nervous system, why safety has to be built before processing can happen, and why rushing any of this is counterproductive.

She will not push you faster than you can go. She will also not let you stay stuck forever out of excessive caution. The work moves at a pace that is genuinely safe and genuinely productive — often both at once.

The memory doesn’t go away. But it loses its grip. It stops feeling like it’s happening now. It becomes something that happened — something you carry, but lighter.
